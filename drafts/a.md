LEAD: The United States moves to forfeit $1.1 billion of USDC stolen from Binance.

The Justice Department filed a civil forfeiture action on 23 November 2026 against USDC it says North Korean actors took from Binance. The stolen total exceeds $6 billion, and the case turns frozen money into government-held money.

The department announced on 23 November 2026 that it is seeking to forfeit over $1.1 billion of USDC, a stablecoin issued by Circle, as proceeds of the theft [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/united-states-files-action-recover-more-1-billion-cryptocurrency-assets-stolen-north-korean]. It says the theft happened on or about 29 October 2026 and exceeded $6 billion [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/united-states-files-action-recover-more-1-billion-cryptocurrency-assets-stolen-north-korean]. The FBI attributes the theft to North Korean military hackers it calls TraderTraitor [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency].

The new filing follows an earlier step. On 12 November 2026 the department said courts had authorized it to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the stolen assets [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency]. Forfeiture is the next stage: the restraint held the money, and the claim asks a court to transfer it. The department says it is part of an effort to help Binance recover the stolen assets, and counts more than $2.9 billion frozen, seized or returned from multiple sources [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/united-states-files-action-recover-more-1-billion-cryptocurrency-assets-stolen-north-korean].

The department's 12 November tally of frozen assets was more than $2.7 billion, so the count rose by about $200 million in 11 days [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency]. crypto.news reports the complaint covers USDC tied to 36 addresses on 18 blockchains and that North Korea has denied involvement [https://crypto.news/binance-hack-usdc-forfeiture/]. The article rounds the claim to about $1 billion, where the department says over $1.1 billion. The primary figure is used here.

Two questions stay open. The department says laundering through bridges, mixers and exchanges is continuing [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency]. And the filing does not settle how much of the roughly $6 billion Binance will get back, since the government's frozen total counts several sources.

NEWS:

[NEWS] South Korea's financial regulator takes up its digital asset law on 24 November 2026.
The Financial Services Commission is due to discuss the Digital Asset Basic Act in its Virtual Asset Committee at 3 p.m. Korea time on 24 November 2026, according to crypto.news, which cites the commission's published schedule [https://crypto.news/south-korea-crypto-law-draft-november-24-stablecoins/]. Yonhap News Agency reported the plan on 22 November 2026 [https://www.yna.co.kr/view/AKR20261121042700002]. Reported options include a cap of about 15% to 20% on major exchange shareholders and a Bank of Korea preference that banks hold at least 50% plus one share of any won stablecoin issuer [https://crypto.news/south-korea-crypto-law-draft-november-24-stablecoins/]. The bill is not law, and the government has not confirmed that every reported provision will appear in the text.

[NEWS] The Justice Department seizes infrastructure behind a North Korean deepfake theft scheme.
On 12 November 2026 the department announced it had seized cloud infrastructure used in a scheme the FBI calls FaceStealer [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency]. The FBI says the scheme stole more than $400 million in 18 months, using fake video meetings with recorded or deepfaked contacts to install malware [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency]. Agents seized over 900 webcam videos of victims under a federal warrant, and the FBI says the scheme now also targets open-source software maintainers [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-announces-actions-disrupt-north-koreas-massive-theft-cryptocurrency].

[NEWS] An Abu Dhabi court order keeps Binance-hack assets frozen pending a 1 December 2026 hearing.
Binance said on 18 November 2026 that it had obtained a court order in Abu Dhabi to help recover frozen assets, according to crypto.news, which cites Reuters [https://crypto.news/binance-hack-usdc-forfeiture/]. The order reportedly directs 18 companies, including Circle and World Liberty Financial, to keep covered assets frozen, and Tether is outside its scope [https://crypto.news/binance-hack-usdc-forfeiture/]. The same report says the court will hold a hearing on 1 December 2026 on whether the restrictions continue. Binance puts its own figure for frozen or intercepted assets at about $2.85 billion, which is a company estimate and not a court finding [https://crypto.news/binance-hack-usdc-forfeiture/].

TECH:

[TECH] Ethereum developers weigh a Glamsterdam delay to May 2027.
At the All Core Developers meeting on 19 November 2026, developers discussed changing Glamsterdam's gas repricing proposals, EIP-8037 and EIP-8038, a change that could push the fork as far as May 2027, Cointelegraph reports [https://cointelegraph.com/news/ethereum-glamsterdam-could-slip-to-may-2027]. ChainSecurity warned in October 2026 that the repricing could expose existing contracts, and the Hoodi testnet fork set for 26 October 2026 was postponed [https://cointelegraph.com/news/ethereum-glamsterdam-could-slip-to-may-2027]. The Ethereum Foundation, ChainSecurity and Dedaub have contacted at least 50 affected companies; three projects rated high impact expect fixes by mid-December, and medium-impact projects run to 10 January 2027 [https://cointelegraph.com/news/ethereum-glamsterdam-could-slip-to-may-2027]. No decision on the specification has been reported.

[TECH] The Hegotá fork's scope stays open as post-quantum compatibility questions surface.
Cointelegraph reports that developers on 19 November 2026 considered postponing some proposals to prioritize post-quantum features in Hegotá, the fork after Glamsterdam [https://cointelegraph.com/news/ethereum-glamsterdam-could-slip-to-may-2027]. Several argued a smaller scope helps only if the rollout also gets shorter [https://cointelegraph.com/news/ethereum-glamsterdam-could-slip-to-may-2027]. A preliminary review found a potentially large number of existing contracts could be affected by EIP-8151, which protects accounts migrating to post-quantum security [https://cointelegraph.com/news/ethereum-glamsterdam-could-slip-to-may-2027]. Client teams are due to submit positions ahead of talks in two weeks. Separately, the developer lightclient argues that frame transactions meet goals such as key rotation and signatures of two to ten kilobytes in a post dated 5 September 2026 [https://lightclient.io/blog/frames-are-all-you-need/].

CULTURE:

[CULTURE] Paradigm's engineers publish a field guide to running swarms of coding agents.
On 20 November 2026 Georgios Konstantopoulos of Paradigm published "Effective Swarm Riding for Engineers" [https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/swarms]. He writes that swarms became practical with models such as Opus 5.5 and urges engineers to rewrite parts of their dependency stack, including databases and cryptography [https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/swarms]. He says Paradigm rebuilt its Tempo node in more than five languages in days [https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/swarms]. These are his claims and the post reports no independent measurement. Rolling your own cryptography is the advice security engineers have long rejected, and the post does not address that dispute.

[CULTURE] Haseeb Qureshi argues that failure's second punishment is a culture choice.
Haseeb Qureshi, managing partner at Dragonfly, wrote that Silicon Valley's distinction is that it does not add social shame to the cost of a failed startup [https://haseebq.com/silicon-valley-doesnt-punish-failure/]. The essay, dated 27 June 2026, reacts to a post mocking a founder who paid himself $212,000 a year before winding down [https://haseebq.com/silicon-valley-doesnt-punish-failure/]. Qureshi calls $200,000 a year about one engineer's salary and notes that seed rounds run to millions [https://haseebq.com/silicon-valley-doesnt-punish-failure/]. The piece is five months old. It is included because it frames how a bear-market wind-down gets judged in public.
